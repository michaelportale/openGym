# Personal strength policy v1

Status: implementation contract for the personal strength work, 2026-10-21. Values below are planning defaults, not a prediction of muscle growth, recovery, injury risk, or a medical prescription. Keep this policy version with generated suggestions so later changes do not reinterpret old decisions. Completed records remain facts; suggestions never rewrite them.

## Evidence boundary

The [2026 ACSM position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) synthesized 137 systematic reviews of randomized resistance-training trials in healthy adults (interventions at least six weeks, generally 6–52 weeks). It found that resistance training improves strength and size, higher volume favors hypertrophy, and strength can benefit from at least two sessions per week. When volume was equated, hypertrophy was not clearly different across frequencies. The paper reports at least 10 sets per muscle group per week as a volume level associated with more hypertrophy in the reviewed comparisons, **not** a minimum for benefit or an individual target. Its authors explicitly could not determine an optimum set count. The full methods and discussion were reviewed via the [open full-text record](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12965823).

[Pelland et al. (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/) analyzed 67 studies (2,058 participants; 79.1% male; mean age 25.16 years). They classified sets relative to the measured muscle or strength outcome, compared indirect-set weights of 0, 0.5, and 1, and found the strongest relative model evidence for 0.5. Volume had diminishing returns; the frequency relationship was clearer for strength than hypertrophy. This is a population-level model comparison, not a validated exercise-by-muscle coefficient or a personal dose calculator. Its publicly accessible abstract supplies these methods; full publisher methods were not accessible during this review. The authors list [data and analysis materials](https://osf.io/6z3xu/), which were not reanalyzed here.

[Robinson et al. (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/) found an exploratory association between lower *estimated* RIR and greater hypertrophy, while the strength association was negligible. They estimated RIR from intervention descriptions rather than observing every set, and the authors caution that the precise relationship remains unclear. The [ACSM review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) found no consistent benefit from taking sets to momentary failure. These findings support recording effort and using it conservatively, not multiplying each set by an invented RIR stimulus factor.

## Coverage and exposure

| Rule | Default and exact interpretation | Basis and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Windows | Display completed work in a trailing seven-day window ending now, plus the active accepted PPL/UL cycle. Use completed timestamps, not planned dates. Show prescribed work separately. | Product requirement; seven days is a reporting window, not an adaptation window. |
| Direct sets | A completed working set contributes `1` to each explicitly identified primary target. | Product accounting convention informed by Pelland's direct classification. Warm-ups, uncompleted sets, and unknown exercise identity do not enter the known count. |
| Secondary sets | A completed working set contributes `0.5` to each explicitly identified relevant secondary target. Display direct, secondary, and `direct + 0.5 × secondary` totals separately. Never infer secondary targets from equipment type alone. | Pelland's fractional model had strongest relative support; `0.5` is an estimate, not a physiological conversion. Do not change the legacy `0.4` used by recovery/history consumers without a separate migration. |
| Ambiguity | If role, target, working-set status, or completion is unknown, report an unknown/unclassified bucket. Do not silently count it as zero or invent a target. Off-program completed work counts once when its target is known. | Data integrity rule. A missing workout log does not prove a missed session. |
| Frequency aim | User-editable planning aim: `2` direct-work days per muscle group in a rolling seven days. A direct-work day means at least `1` completed, non-warm-up working set with that muscle explicitly marked primary; count at most once per muscle per local training day. Show days with secondary work but no direct work separately. Compare the observed direct-day count with the aim as a schedule indicator only. | User planning preference and transparent bookkeeping definition; strength frequency signal in ACSM/Pelland. Hypertrophy advantage at equal volume is uncertain. One set is **not** a validated minimum effective exposure. |
| Meaningful exposure | **Scientifically unresolved.** No numeric set or RIR threshold is approved for calling an exposure physiologically “meaningful.” Never convert the direct-day comparison into a “fully trained,” “sufficient stimulus,” or catch-up verdict. Show volume and exercise role alongside frequency, with any shortfall described in those observed terms. | Neither cited synthesis validates a universal per-session qualifying threshold. A future calibrated threshold needs a new policy version, not a hidden constant. |
| Volume interpretation | Show counts and trends; do not label `<10` fractional sets deficient or `≥10` optimal. Any target is user-editable and visually distinct from observed volume. | ACSM's `≥10` finding concerns group comparisons, and Pelland reports diminishing returns, not an individual minimum. |

Example: three completed working sets of a press with chest as primary and triceps as a documented secondary add chest direct `3`, triceps secondary `3`, and triceps fractional `1.5`. They create one direct-work day for chest and one secondary-only day for triceps. Neither count certifies a sufficient stimulus. If only the exercise category is known, the target assignment remains unknown. A set never receives both direct and secondary credit for the same muscle.

## Effort-aware next-session progression

The following exact numbers are **editable product heuristics**, not thresholds established by the studies. Use only completed comparable work: same exercise identity, units, equipment station/load convention (including plate-loaded per-side), movement variant, and prescribed rep range. Do not compare a per-hand dumbbell load with a barbell total, a machine setting with kilograms, or a changed station as if they were the same lift. Preserve the exercise/equipment snapshot used for each observation.

1. For a new or non-comparable lift, offer an editable starting prescription using the existing plan or last clearly comparable history. Say when no comparable history exists. Do not compute a progression verdict from unknown identity or load convention.
2. For double progression, retain the plan's rep range. If **all prescribed working sets were completed** at one comparable load, each reached the range's upper rep bound, and the **last working set's logged RIR is at least 2**, suggest the smallest known available load step in the same convention, then return to the lower rep bound. Show the proposed load and percentage change. The `2 RIR` gate and one-session trigger are conservative application choices. Do not claim that earlier sets had `≥2 RIR`.
3. If the final set has `0–1 RIR`, retain load and offer reps within the range next time. If the final set is `≥4 RIR` but reps have not reached the upper bound on every working set, suggest reps first. If a documented safety concern, pain, technique problem, or user override exists, avoid an automatic increase and expose the reason. These are coaching heuristics, not measured adaptation.
4. If last-set RIR is absent but all prescribed working sets were completed at the same comparable load and reached the upper bound, show only a **limited, editable reps-based load suggestion** explicitly marked “effort unknown”; never say the effort gate passed. If any required rep or load is missing, make no load increase recommendation. Never copy the last-set rating to earlier sets or substitute an estimated RIR for a logged one.
5. Do not force a load increase when no exact increment is known. The user may accept, edit, or dismiss a routine suggestion. A meaningful future-plan change requires a separate preview and acceptance. Two consecutive comparable underperforming sessions may prompt a review, but do not diagnose a plateau, prescribe a deload, or decrease load automatically.

The [older ACSM progression stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/) describes incremental increases in load/repetitions as ways to progress, but the [2026 update](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12965823) emphasizes individualization and does not validate this app's `2 RIR` gate, one-session trigger, or equipment step size. Show the reason for each suggestion and keep it editable.

## Session duration and adaptation

| Rule | Default | Basis and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Schedule | Stable PPL repeated across six available sessions; when availability falls to five, propose PPL plus upper/lower. Do not infer availability from a missing log. | Accepted product behavior, not a frequency result from research. |
| Budget | Aim for `75 min`; show `60–90 min` as the usual planning band. If estimated work exceeds the available time, surface the shortfall and ask which work to defer. Do not compress all missed work into the remaining sessions. | User preference; no trial establishes a personal 75-minute optimum. |
| Rest | Planning estimate: `150 s` between working sets of a multi-joint lift, `90 s` between sets of a single-joint lift, both editable. Do not add inter-set rest after the final set of an exercise. | [Singer et al. (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349676/) analyzed nine randomized studies; results overlap across rest durations, with a possible small advantage above 60 seconds and uncertain value beyond 90 seconds. These values are scheduling assumptions, not recovery mandates. |
| Set and transition time | Estimate `45 s` per working set and `120 s` to change exercises or stations; include any explicitly planned warm-up time. Sum components and show them so the user can revise the estimate. | Product heuristic, unvalidated for this user and especially variable in a busy gym. |
| Adaptation | Treat progress as observed change across comparable sessions over weeks, with no guaranteed response date. Never infer adaptation from one session or use a countdown for muscle recovery. Review targets and prescriptions in light of actual trend and user feedback; keep historical prescriptions and completions distinct. | ACSM's evidence comes from interventions of at least six weeks and emphasizes individual variation; group averages do not provide an individual timetable. |

This policy is independent of the existing fatigue model. Its 0.4 secondary factor, estimated effort, and recovery state must not be silently reused as evidence that an exposure qualified or a load increase is safe. Any future integration needs an explicit versioned decision and tests against synthetic examples.
